Healthy weight gain generally involves consuming more calories than the body uses, often called a calorie surplus, combined with adequate protein intake and resistance training to support muscle growth rather than only fat gain.

A gradual approach to weight gain, such as a modest calorie surplus, is often recommended over a large surplus, since gaining weight too quickly can lead to a higher proportion of fat gain relative to muscle.

Protein is especially important for those aiming to gain weight through muscle growth, since it provides the building blocks for muscle repair and growth. Sources include meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes, tofu, and protein-rich grains like quinoa.

Calorie-dense, nutrient-rich foods can make it easier to reach a calorie surplus without eating excessive volumes of food. Examples include nuts and nut butters, avocado, olive oil, whole grains, dried fruit, and full-fat dairy.

Frequent, balanced meals and snacks throughout the day can help some people reach higher calorie intakes more comfortably than trying to eat very large meals only two or three times a day.

Resistance training, such as weightlifting or bodyweight exercises, signals the body to direct extra calories toward building muscle tissue rather than only storing fat. Progressive overload, or gradually increasing the challenge of workouts over time, supports continued muscle growth.

Adequate sleep and recovery time between workouts are important for muscle repair and growth, since much of this repair process happens during rest rather than during exercise itself.

Smoothies and shakes combining ingredients like milk, yogurt, nut butter, fruit, and oats can be a convenient way to add calories and nutrients for people who struggle to eat large meals.

Some individuals have a naturally fast metabolism, higher activity levels, or reduced appetite due to stress, illness, or other factors, which can make gaining weight more challenging and may benefit from professional guidance.

Tracking progress for healthy weight gain is often done gradually over weeks, since day-to-day body weight can fluctuate due to water retention, digestion, and other factors unrelated to actual tissue gain.

Consulting a registered dietitian or healthcare provider is especially recommended for individuals trying to gain weight due to underlying health conditions, significant unintentional weight loss, or difficulty gaining weight through diet alone.

Weight gain that is unintentional or rapid, or accompanied by other symptoms, can sometimes signal an underlying medical issue and is worth discussing with a healthcare provider.
